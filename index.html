Learning tools — picked for your specific goals

Indonesian

Pimsleur for pronunciation and listening habit, paired with Anki (spaced-repetition flashcards) for vocab retention, plus italki for real conversation with a native tutor once you've got a base. This three-part combo — audio habit, spaced recall, live use — is the combination language educators most consistently point to, rather than any single app doing it all.

Japanese

Same structure, adjusted for kanji: WaniKani (purpose-built spaced repetition for kanji/vocab) is the standout tool language communities rate highest for kanji specifically, Anki for everything else, italki for speaking, and Migaku if you want to learn from native shows/YouTube later on.

App building (learn to make apps for your phone)

Two honest paths — pick based on what you actually want: if the goal is "get an app working," start with a no-code builder like FlutterFlow (visual builder, exports to real Flutter code if you outgrow it later) or Thunkable. If the goal is "actually learn to code," Swift Playgrounds (free, Apple-made, genuinely beginner-friendly) or freeCodeCamp for programming fundamentals first.

Cabinetry — drawing plans (Job 1 & 2)

SketchUp Free is the practical pick for drawing cabinetry plans — free, browser-based, and the closest thing to an industry standard for quick furniture/joinery drafting without a steep CAD learning curve.

Surfing — local waves around Miami

Miami Beach itself is a patrolled beach break with A-frame peaks, better left than right, and usually less crowded than the points nearby — worth starting there. Burleigh Heads, just south, is a serious barrelling point break for experienced surfers, not a place to learn. Broadbeach, north of Miami, is the more forgiving, beginner-friendly option; further south, Currumbin ("The Alley") is longboard-friendly. For day-to-day conditions, a live swell/report app (Coastalwatch or Swellnet) will tell you more than any static guide.

Gym & nutrition

The consistent, evidence-backed thread across strength research: resistance training at least twice a week with progressive overload is the single biggest lever for long-term strength, muscle, and healthy aging — bigger than any specific program. Nutrition-wise, adequate protein (commonly cited around 1.6–2.2g/kg bodyweight for people strength training) paired with mostly whole foods covers most of what the longevity research agrees on. This is general, well-established guidance, not a personal program — worth a chat with a doctor or accredited exercise physiologist for anything tailored to you.

Sources: NIA — strength training & aging · ACE Fitness — strength training for longevity · Surf Atlas — Gold Coast guide
Learning systems & mindsets
  • Spaced repetition — review something right before you'd forget it, not on a fixed schedule. This is why Anki/WaniKani beat flashcard cramming for anything you need to retain long-term.
  • Deliberate practice — focused reps just past your current skill level, with feedback, beat unfocused hours logged. 20 sharp minutes on one weak point outperforms 2 hours of comfortable repetition.
  • Growth mindset (Carol Dweck) — treat "I'm bad at this" as "I'm bad at this yet." Ability grows with the right kind of effort; being new at cabinetry, Japanese, or the gym is a starting point, not a verdict.
  • Active recall & interleaving — testing yourself (not re-reading notes) and mixing skills in a session both beat passive review for anything you actually need to remember or apply under pressure.
Positive & realistic thinking

The version of "positive thinking" with the best track record isn't blind optimism — it's realistic optimism: expect the thing to be genuinely hard, plan for the setback, and still believe it's workable. Positive psychology research (Seligman and others) consistently finds this beats both pessimism and unrealistic "it'll all work out" thinking, because it doesn't collapse the first time something goes wrong.

Practical version for this tracker: before starting an item, name one likely snag out loud, then start anyway. It removes the surprise, not the motivation.

Motivation that doesn't go stale

The research (Self-Determination Theory — Deci & Ryan; popularized by Daniel Pink's Drive) is consistent: motivation holds up longest when three things are present — autonomy (you chose the how), mastery (visible progress, however small), and purpose (it connects to something bigger than the task). A single static motivational quote or poster fades fast because it only ever hits one of these.

That's why the banner on the Today tab rotates daily instead of showing the same line — pulling from growth-mindset, autonomy/mastery/purpose, realistic-optimism, and a couple of other angles, so it stays a nudge rather than wallpaper.

Sources: Autonomy, Mastery, Purpose — Pink's Drive · MindTools — AMP framework
Motivational items

Physical, visible anchors tend to outlast digital ones. A few worth considering against your list above: a small SketchUp print-out or sketch of the finished cabinetry piece pinned where you work; a "16 houses built" reminder somewhere in the workshop for the days a job feels slow; a simple physical tally (tokens, a jar, a wall chart) for gym or surf sessions so progress is visible without opening an app; a single Indonesian/Japanese word-of-the-day card on the fridge.

Quantum considerations

If you want to actually learn quantum physics: for a first real pass, Sean Carroll's Something Deeply Hidden or Leonard Susskind's Theoretical Minimum series (book or free Stanford lectures) are the commonly recommended entry points — the first for concepts with no maths, the second if you want the actual mechanics.

Quantum-inspired mindset framing: some self-development writing borrows quantum terms — "observer effect," "superposition," "possibility" — as metaphors for mindset (e.g. "how you observe a situation shapes what you notice in it"). Worth being clear-eyed about: this is metaphor, not physics — actual quantum mechanics doesn't operate on human intention or belief, and treating it as if it does is a popular but scientifically unsupported leap. Used honestly as metaphor rather than mechanism, though, "what you focus on is what you notice" is a fair and useful idea on its own.

Breathing techniques
  • Physiological sigh (double inhale through the nose, long exhale through the mouth) — the fastest-acting of the three for calming down in the moment; good right before a hard conversation or a stressful task.
  • Box breathing (inhale–hold–exhale–hold, 4 seconds each) — used by military/tactical training for steady, sustained calm and focus; good pre-session ritual before gym, surfing, or a big pricing job.
  • Wim Hof method (controlled hyperventilation + breath holds, often paired with cold exposure) — more about building resilience/energy over time than instant calm; evidence is promising but less settled than the other two, and it's not the one to reach for mid-stress.
Laws of karma

Stripped to its practical core (from the Buddhist/Hindu framing this idea comes from): actions — including intent, not just the deed — tend to shape what comes back to you over time. Used as a personal mindset rather than a literal cosmic ledger, it's a useful accountability lens: the effort you put into a job, a relationship, or a habit today is genuinely what tends to compound into tomorrow's outcome. That's the version worth keeping close to this tracker — cause and effect, intent counted, patience with the timeline.

Sources: Tricycle — The Law of Karma · Buddhanet — The Law of Karma
